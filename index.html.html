The case

Both halves, in the same person.

Most candidates on a Chief AI Officer slate have one of two halves. They are technologists with genuine depth who have never carried an enterprise remit, or they are executives with scope who cannot evaluate what their teams are building.

Eric Brown has both, in a regulated industry, and the transition between them is documented rather than claimed. He wrote production code, then climbed through architecture, engineering management, and a full-stack two-theater CIO seat to a remit that now explicitly names enterprise AI strategy.

More consequentially, he shipped machine learning into a regulated medical device company in 2021 and has since run a working model for governed AI adoption — the problem most organizations are still writing policy about.

“Regional CIO isn’t enterprise CIO.”

+

Fair, but narrower than it first appears. Americas plus EMEA with full stack ownership is most of a global remit, and the headcount and country footprint support it. What remains genuinely untested is the global seat itself and a direct line to the board or audit committee. Test that specifically rather than treating the whole dimension as unproven.

“Was the 2021–23 role Americas, or Americas and EMEA?”

+

Unresolved, and worth clarifying before a partner finds it. Two independent 2022 sources — the Foundry speaker biography and the Stevie award credits — give his title as VP, Americas Technology. His profile describes the same period as Regional CIO for Americas and EMEA.

The likeliest explanation is that EMEA was added during the tenure and the profile describes the end state while the 2022 sources describe the middle. That is ordinary. But scope is the single most scrutinised claim in a CIO search, so the date EMEA joined his remit should be confirmed and stated plainly rather than left for someone else to discover.

“Ten and a half years at one company — depth or insularity?”

+

Depth, on the evidence. Four distinct roles with materially different centers of gravity. Someone entrenched does not get moved from a geographic remit into a newly-defined AI strategy mandate. The residual risk is portability. Everything known about how he operates comes from one company’s culture, and Earned Access in particular was built for an organization that already accepted regulated-industry discipline. Worth testing how he would install it somewhere less patient.

“Is the award work actually his?”

+

Independently verified. The Stevie public record lists Eric second on the Insight 360 executive credits, directly beneath the SVP and Chief Digital Officer. This does not rest on anyone’s account — it is a third-party published record naming him.

The honest nuance: he was the technology executive on it, not the top executive. A Chief Digital Officer sat above him. A partner who pulls the same record will see that, so present it that way rather than claiming sole ownership. Second name on a Gold Stevie with quantified outcomes is a strong position without any inflation.

“Where is he actually weakest?”

+

One place, narrower than it looks, plus one unknown. Board-level narration of AI risk has no observed instance. He speaks to peer CIO audiences, sits on three advisory or governance boards, and publishes a defensible point of view on enterprise AI governance — but addressing a board that controls risk appetite is a different performance from any of those.

Separately, cybersecurity ownership is unevidenced and traces to never having held the enterprise seat. Neither is disqualifying for a CAIO mandate. Both are worth a direct question rather than an inference.
